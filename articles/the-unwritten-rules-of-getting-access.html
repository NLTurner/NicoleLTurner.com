WORK + OPPORTUNITY · WRITING + OBSERVATIONS

The Unwritten Rules of Getting Access

We've all seen it happen.

Someone gets promoted. Someone lands a job that was never publicly advertised. A business owner secures a contract after being introduced to the right person. Someone receives an invitation that puts them in a room with people who can change the trajectory of their career.

And sometimes, when you look at the people who received those opportunities, you wonder what distinguished them from others who were just as qualified.

The answer isn't always experience, education, talent, or performance.

Sometimes it's who they know.

More importantly, it's who knows them.

The Advantage of Being Known

Think about how many opportunities begin with a conversation.

An executive mentions that the organization is considering creating a new position. Someone asks whether anybody knows a consultant who could help with a particular problem. A board member is asked to recommend someone for an appointment.

Names come up.

Usually, they're the names of people someone already knows, has worked with, or has heard good things about.

The people whose names never enter the conversation may have everything the opportunity requires. But they aren't part of the conversation, and they may never know it took place.

That's a significant advantage for people who have relationships with individuals who make decisions or influence them.

And it's a disadvantage for people who don't.

Let's Talk About Likability

We don't talk enough about how much likability influences professional opportunities.

People tend to prefer working with people they like. They recommend people they enjoy being around. They invite people into conversations and environments where they feel comfortable.

Think about the colleague who is always invited to participate in high-profile projects. The employee whose supervisor takes a personal interest in their career. The business owner who gets introduced to potential clients because people enjoy talking with them.

Certainly, those individuals may be talented and deserving.

But likability can give someone an advantage that qualifications alone don't provide.

And likability is subjective.

A person who is outgoing, personable, and comfortable making conversation may be viewed more favorably than someone who is reserved, even when both are equally competent.

Someone who shares interests or experiences with a decision-maker may find it easier to establish a relationship.

We also know that people don't always respond to others without bias. What one person considers confident, another may consider arrogant. What one person considers direct, another may consider difficult.

These judgments can influence who gets invited, recommended, promoted, or overlooked.

That's why the idea that professional success is determined entirely by merit deserves more scrutiny.

Two People. Similar Qualifications. Different Opportunities.

Imagine two employees who have worked for the same organization for several years.

Both perform well. Both have the experience required for a management position. Both would be credible candidates for promotion.

One has developed relationships with senior leaders. Those leaders know the person's work, have observed them in different settings, and feel comfortable recommending them.

The other employee has spent years producing excellent results but has had limited interaction with anyone outside their immediate department.

When a promotional opportunity becomes available, the first employee already has people who can speak on their behalf.

The second may have to rely almost entirely on an application and a résumé.

Neither person necessarily did anything wrong.

But one entered the process with an advantage that had been developing long before the position became available.

The same thing happens in business.

Two consultants may offer comparable services. One receives a call because a potential client heard their name from a trusted colleague. The other spends months responding to solicitations and trying to get an introductory meeting.

A relationship can eliminate obstacles that someone else has to work much harder to overcome.

When Opportunity Changes Everything

What makes access so consequential is what can happen after someone receives it.

A promotion provides new responsibilities, visibility, and experience that qualify someone for future positions.

A business contract generates revenue, establishes a client relationship, and creates a reference that may help secure additional work.

An invitation to serve on a board introduces someone to people they might never have encountered otherwise.

One opportunity can lead to another, and then another.

Over time, the difference between two equally qualified people can become substantial.

We may eventually look at their careers and conclude that one was more successful because they were more talented, ambitious, or prepared.

Perhaps.

But we rarely know how many opportunities each person had access to along the way.

And that matters when we use someone's accomplishments as evidence of what they deserved.

What You Know Still Matters

I wouldn't suggest that expertise, competence, and performance have become irrelevant.

They haven't.

Relationships may get someone considered for an opportunity, but knowledge and performance often determine what happens after they receive it.

People who consistently fail to deliver can damage their reputations, regardless of how many people they know.

Still, we should acknowledge something that doesn't always fit comfortably with what we've been taught about success.

Being good at what you do doesn&#x27;t guarantee that the people who could create opportunities for you will ever know you exist.

And being known can sometimes provide an advantage over someone who is equally good, or even better, at the work.

That's a difficult reality for people who have spent years believing their accomplishments would eventually speak for themselves.

Sometimes they do.

Often, someone has to be in a position to hear them.

Who Gets to Enter the Room?

There is another part of this conversation that deserves attention.

Not everyone begins with the same access to relationships.

Some people grow up around professionals, business owners, executives, and community leaders. They learn how certain environments operate because they've been exposed to them for years.

Others may be the first in their families to enter a profession, start a business, or pursue a leadership position.

They may have the education, intelligence, and determination to succeed without knowing anyone who can explain the unwritten rules.

They may not know which organizations matter, which events are worth attending, or how certain opportunities are actually filled.

And some of the most influential relationships develop in environments that require money, time, or introductions simply to participate.

This doesn't mean every opportunity is unfairly awarded.

It does mean that access isn't distributed equally, even among people with comparable qualifications.

The Part We Can Change

I think people who have benefited from access have an opportunity to consider how they use it.

When someone asks for a recommendation, do we immediately think of the people we know best? Are there qualified individuals whose work deserves consideration but who aren't part of our usual circles?

When we receive an invitation, is there someone else who might benefit from being introduced?

When we're in positions to make decisions, are we evaluating people based on what they can contribute, or are we allowing familiarity and personal comfort to carry more weight than they should?

These aren't always easy questions.

Relationships are a natural part of life, and there's nothing inherently wrong with wanting to work with people we know and like.

But it's possible to value relationships while recognizing that they can also create barriers for others.

I don't believe the answer is to pretend relationships shouldn't matter.

They do, and they always have.

What concerns me is how easily we can mistake access for merit, or assume that people who haven't advanced simply haven't done enough.

Two people can work equally hard, possess comparable talent, and make similar sacrifices without ever receiving comparable opportunities.

One may eventually be described as exceptionally successful.

The other may still be waiting for someone to recognize what they have to offer.

And sometimes the biggest difference between them is that someone opened a door for one and never knew the other was standing outside.
