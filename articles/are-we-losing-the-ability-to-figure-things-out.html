Are We Losing the Ability to Figure Things Out?

Remember when you had to figure out how to get somewhere?

You might have written directions on a piece of paper, printed them from MapQuest, or kept a road atlas in your car. If you missed a turn, you had to figure out where you were and how to get back on track. And if you stopped to ask someone for directions, there was always the possibility they'd tell you to turn left at the old gas station that had been demolished ten years earlier.

Now, a pleasant voice tells us exactly where to go. Miss a turn, and it recalculates without making us feel bad about it.

I appreciate that. I have no desire to return to the days of trying to read directions while driving.

But I've been wondering whether all this convenience has changed something about us.

Are we losing the ability, or perhaps the willingness, to figure things out for ourselves?

There Was a Time When You Had to Try

Think about the things we used to do without immediately reaching for a phone.

You bought a piece of furniture and had to figure out how to assemble it. You tried a new recipe without watching someone demonstrate every step. You encountered a problem at work and spent time considering possible solutions before asking someone else.

Sometimes you got it wrong. Sometimes you made things worse. And occasionally, you surprised yourself by figuring out something you didn't know you could do.

Today, there's a tutorial for almost everything.

Need to replace a faucet? There's a video.

Can't remember how to calculate a percentage? There's a calculator.

Wondering what to say in a difficult conversation? Artificial intelligence can draft the entire exchange.

These tools save time, reduce frustration, and make knowledge accessible to people who might otherwise struggle to obtain it.

I'm not suggesting we abandon them. If there's a video showing me how to fix something in five minutes instead of spending two hours trying to figure it out, I'm probably watching the video.

But I do wonder what happens when looking for an answer becomes our first response to almost everything.

Have We Become Less Comfortable Not Knowing?

I remember when you could have an entire conversation trying to remember the name of an actor or a movie.

Someone would offer a guess. Another person would insist that couldn't be right. Eventually, somebody would remember a completely unrelated detail that helped everyone figure it out.

Now someone picks up a phone, and the conversation is over in twelve seconds.

We got the answer, but we missed the experience of trying to remember.

That may seem insignificant. And in that particular situation, it probably is.

But consider how often we bypass the process of thinking through something because an answer is readily available.

There is value in struggling with a problem long enough to understand it. We learn to make connections, evaluate possibilities, and recognize when something doesn't make sense.

Those habits develop through use.

If we rarely practice them, what happens when we encounter a situation that doesn't come with instructions?

And Then Came Artificial Intelligence

AI has taken convenience to another level.

We can ask it to write an email, develop a business idea, plan a vacation, analyze information, or recommend what to do about a problem.

I've found it useful for exploring ideas and examining possibilities. It can introduce considerations I hadn't thought about and help organize information that would otherwise take considerable time to assemble.

But I've also noticed how easy it is to accept an answer simply because it's presented confidently and sounds reasonable.

An AI-generated response can be polished, detailed, and completely wrong.

That means we still need to know enough to question what we're being told.

And that raises another concern.

If someone consistently relies on AI to make decisions, formulate arguments, and solve problems, how do they develop the judgment necessary to recognize a bad recommendation?

I think this is an important question for educators, employers, parents, and anyone using these tools.

Knowing how to obtain an answer isn't the same as knowing whether the answer makes sense.

Maybe We're Just Figuring Out Different Things

Of course, every generation has benefited from inventions that reduced the amount of effort required to perform certain tasks.

Calculators didn't eliminate mathematics. Washing machines didn't eliminate the need to care for clothing. And few people would argue that we should give up modern medicine so we can become better at treating ourselves.

Technology has always changed which skills we use and how we use them.

Perhaps what's happening now is another version of that transition.

Instead of memorizing information, we may spend more time evaluating it. Instead of completing routine tasks manually, we may concentrate on more complicated problems.

That's a reasonable possibility.

But it assumes we're actually using the time and mental effort we've saved to do something that requires thought.

Are we?

Or are we simply becoming accustomed to having fewer things demand it?

I don't know that there's one answer.

I do know that some of the most satisfying experiences in life come from figuring something out that initially seemed beyond our ability.

There's a particular feeling that comes from solving a problem, repairing something, learning a skill, or finding your way through an unfamiliar situation without someone providing every step.

I'd hate for us to lose that.

And I have to admit, there's something amusing about the possibility that we may eventually need an AI tutorial to teach us how to figure things out without AI.
