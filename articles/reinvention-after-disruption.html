Reinvention After Disruption

Losing a job can make you question more than where your next paycheck is coming from.

It can make you question what you know.

For years, maybe decades, you built expertise in a particular organization, profession, industry, or sector. You knew how things worked. You understood the language. You had relationships. You had a track record.

Then the job disappeared.

Now you're looking at opportunities that don't look quite like what you used to do. Maybe you're considering a different industry. Maybe the kind of position you held isn't as plentiful as it once was. Maybe changes in technology, funding, government, or the economy have forced you to consider options you never expected to consider.

And suddenly, experience that once made you feel established can start to feel strangely specific.

Who needs what I know outside of this industry?

How do I convince someone that I can do something different?

Am I really qualified for that?

What am I supposed to do with everything I've spent all these years learning?

Here's where I think we sometimes get reinvention wrong.

We sometimes confuse where we gained our expertise with where that expertise can be valuable.

The organization where you worked gave you a place to develop and apply what you know. Your job title described a particular role. Your industry gave you specialized knowledge and experience.

But none of those things represents the full value of what you've accumulated along the way.

Your knowledge, judgment, expertise, relationships, pattern recognition, and capabilities don't disappear because the job did.

What do you know how to do?

When people begin thinking about a career change, one of the first pieces of advice they usually hear is to identify their transferable skills.

That's useful, but I think we need to go deeper.

Most experienced professionals have accumulated far more than a list of skills.

You've learned things.

You've seen things.

You've solved things.

You know what tends to go wrong.

You know what questions to ask.

You recognize patterns that someone with less experience might miss.

You know how decisions get made, how people respond to change, how projects go off track, how customers behave, how organizations operate, how relationships get built, or how complicated situations get untangled.

Some of what you know has become so natural to you that you may no longer recognize it as expertise.

That's why the better question isn't simply:

What skills do I have?

It's:

What do I know how to do because of everything I've done?

Those are two very different inventories.

Your experience may travel better than you think

Imagine someone who has spent 20 years working in the federal government.

If she defines the value of those 20 years primarily by the fact that she worked in government, moving into the private sector can feel like starting over.

But suppose those years taught her how to navigate complex regulations, manage competing stakeholders, lead large initiatives, work within constrained budgets, communicate with senior decision-makers, evaluate risk, interpret policy, and get things accomplished inside complicated systems.

Those capabilities don't suddenly become irrelevant when she walks out of a federal building.

The environment changes.

The application changes.

The language may change.

What she knows how to do is still there.

The same is true for someone leaving financial services for higher education, healthcare for technology, corporate America for a nonprofit, or a longtime employee deciding to build something of their own.

You may have to learn a new industry.

You may have to translate your experience differently.

You may even have to accept that some of what you know won't transfer at all.

That's part of reinvention too.

But different is not the same as starting from zero.

Translation matters

One of the hardest parts of reinvention is that other people may not automatically see the connection between what you've done and what you could do next.

You see the connection because you lived the experience.

They didn't.

If your résumé, LinkedIn profile, or conversations rely heavily on the language of your previous environment, people may see you only in that environment.

A federal employee can sound like a federal employee.

A teacher can sound like a teacher.

A nonprofit executive can sound like a nonprofit executive.

A longtime corporate leader can sound like someone who belongs only in a large corporation.

Reinvention requires learning how to describe your experience in terms of the value it creates, not just the setting in which you created it.

"I managed X program" tells me what you did.

"I know how to bring multiple stakeholders with competing priorities together and move a complex initiative forward" tells me something about what you can do elsewhere.

That's the translation.

You aren't pretending your experience was something it wasn't. You're helping someone understand how what you've already done could be useful somewhere else.

You may have to let go of some things

This part doesn't get discussed enough.

Reinvention isn't only about carrying things forward.

Sometimes it's about deciding what doesn't need to come with you.

A title.

A certain level of status.

An identity you've had for years.

The expectation that your next career should make perfect sense when compared with your last one.

Maybe even the belief that because you've spent 20 years doing something, you're supposed to spend the next 10 doing some version of the same thing.

That's difficult because work becomes part of how we explain ourselves.

What do you do?

For years, you had an answer.

Then disruption comes along and suddenly the answer isn't as simple.

But perhaps that uncertainty creates an opening.

Instead of asking:

How do I find another version of what I had?

You get to ask:

Given everything I know now, what else could I do with it?

That's a much bigger question.

Reinvention doesn't erase what came before

There is a tendency to tell people going through career disruption to look forward.

And eventually, yes.

But I don't think reinvention requires dismissing what came before.

Those years mattered.

The expertise mattered.

The relationships mattered.

The mistakes mattered.

The things you figured out the hard way mattered.

Even the work you never want to do again taught you something.

You don't have to recreate your old career to make those years valuable.

You get to decide what you want to carry forward, what needs to be translated, what you're ready to leave behind, and what you want to learn next.

A job can end.

An industry can change.

A profession can be disrupted.

An organization can decide it no longer needs your position.

None of those things gets to retroactively erase what you know.

The question now is what else you can do with it.
